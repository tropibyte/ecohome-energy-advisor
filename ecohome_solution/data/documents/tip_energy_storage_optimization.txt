# Home Battery and Energy Storage Optimization

A home battery shifts energy in time: it charges when electricity is cheap or solar is abundant and discharges when electricity is expensive. It also provides backup power during outages.

## Key specifications
- Usable capacity (kWh): how much energy it stores. A Tesla Powerwall 2, for example, stores 13.5 kWh usable.
- Continuous power (kW): how much it can deliver at once (about 5 kW for a Powerwall 2). Power limits which loads it can run simultaneously; an EV charger plus air conditioner can exceed it.
- Round-trip efficiency: lithium-ion home batteries typically return about 85-90% of the energy stored.
- Chemistry: lithium iron phosphate (LFP) batteries tolerate more cycles (often several thousand) than nickel-based chemistries.

## Operating modes
- Self-powered / self-consumption: store surplus solar during the day and use it in the evening. Best when exports earn little (for example under NEM 3.0).
- Time-based control / TOU arbitrage: charge from solar (or from the grid off-peak if allowed) and discharge during the on-peak window, typically 16:00-21:00.
- Backup reserve: keep a reserve (for example 20-30%) for outages; raise it before storms, wildfire-season power shutoffs or forecast grid emergencies.

## Daily strategy for a solar home on time-of-use rates
1. Morning: the battery serves the small overnight and morning load or holds charge.
2. 10:00-15:00: solar charges the battery to full while also running flexible loads.
3. 16:00-21:00 (peak): the battery covers household load so the home imports little or nothing at peak prices.
4. After 22:00: remaining load comes from cheap off-peak grid power; EV charging happens here.

## When does grid charging make sense?
- Only if the price difference between off-peak and peak exceeds the round-trip loss: with 90% efficiency, charging at $0.22 and discharging to avoid $0.53 saves about $0.53 - 0.22/0.9 = $0.29 per kWh.
- Check your interconnection and incentive rules; some programs restrict grid charging for batteries that received solar incentives.

## Estimating savings
- Daily arbitrage value ≈ usable kWh shifted x (peak rate - export value / round-trip efficiency).
- Example: shifting 12 kWh a day of solar that would have been exported at $0.05 into the peak at $0.53 is worth about 12 x (0.53 - 0.05/0.9) ≈ $5.70 per weekday. Weekend evenings are billed at mid-peak, so annual value is somewhat lower than 365 x the weekday figure.
- Payback period = installed cost after incentives / annual savings. Check current federal, state and utility incentives, which change frequently.

## Battery health tips
- Avoid keeping the battery at 100% for long periods unless a storm or outage is expected.
- Keep it in a shaded, ventilated location; heat accelerates aging.
- Update firmware; manufacturers improve the charging logic.

## EVs as storage
- An EV battery (60-100 kWh) is several times larger than a home battery. Vehicle-to-home (V2H) capable cars and chargers can back up a house for days.
- Even without V2H, scheduling EV charging into solar or off-peak hours is effectively "storage" because the car absorbs cheap energy for later driving.
